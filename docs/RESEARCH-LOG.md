# Research log

Dated findings that shape the rules. Newest first. Each entry: question, finding, strength of evidence, sources, implications.

## 2026-11-05: Does holding a cash reserve to buy dips beat investing it straight away?
- Question: the plan keeps 15% of the ISA as a dip reserve in cash, deployed a third at each confirmed buy tier (-10%, -15/-20%, -30% with fear confirmation), with half moving to the core if unused after 12 months. What does the evidence say about the cost of waiting for dips?
- Finding: Waiting usually costs money. (1) AQR's "Hold the Dip" (2025, via The Evidence-Based Investor) tested 196 buy-the-dip rules on the S&P 500, Jan 1965 to Sep 2025 (dips of 5% to 20%, waits of a week to a year): over 60% had worse risk-adjusted returns than buy-and-hold, and against regular investing the dip-waiting approach ended with 18.7% less wealth. (2) Estrada (IESE, "Buy the Dip? Not So Fast", 2026) used S&P 500 data since 1871 and found buying dips from a bond reserve "rarely" improved risk-adjusted returns versus simple rebalancing or buy-and-hold; differences were small and not significant. (3) Felix and Warwick (PWL Capital, 2021) found "no compelling evidence" for waiting across six countries and MSCI World; buying the dip trailed a lump sum "by a wide margin" over 10-year periods. (4) Maggiulli (Of Dollars and Data, 2019): even with perfect knowledge of every market bottom, buying the dip lost to steady monthly investing over 70% of 40-year windows (1920 to 2019), and 97% of the time if each bottom was missed by two months. It won mainly when a deep bear market came early (the 1929 to 1932 crash).
- Strength: Strong that a standing cash reserve waiting for dips lowers expected long-run returns (several independent studies, long US history, consistent with the lump-sum evidence logged 30 Oct). Moderate on the size of the drag for our exact rule (most studies are US-only; none tests a 15% reserve with a 12-month release).
- Sources: [The Evidence-Based Investor, "Buy the dip" (summary of AQR, "Hold the Dip", 2025)](https://www.evidenceinvestor.com/post/buy-the-dip); [Estrada, "Buy the Dip? Not So Fast" (IESE, 2026)](https://blog.iese.edu/jestrada/files/2026/06/BTD-USA.pdf); [Felix and Warwick, "Buy the Dip" (PWL Capital, 2021)](https://pwlcapital.com/buy-the-dip/); [Maggiulli, "Even God Couldn't Beat Dollar-Cost Averaging" (2019)](https://ofdollarsanddata.com/even-god-couldnt-beat-dollar-cost-averaging/).
- Implication: the dip reserve is best understood as insurance for behaviour (it gives a calm plan for a crash and makes it easier to stay invested), not as a return enhancer. Rough cost at today's settings: if equities beat cash by 3% to 4% a year, keeping 15% in cash drags the whole portfolio by about 0.5% a year while it sits idle. Proposed, not applied: (a) shorten the release so unused reserve drips into the core over the next 6 months instead of half after 12, or (b) cut the reserve to 10% and add the 5% to the core; either way keep the tiers so a real crash still has dry powder. Decision for Douglas; the buy-tier backtest (roadmap) can put a number on the drag with our own rules.

## 2026-11-04: Does buying a stock well below its 52-week high improve long-term returns?
- Question: the long-term score's Entry pillar (26% of the score, and "Good entry" needs Entry 70+) gives the most points to stocks 20% to 30% below their 52-week high, below their 200-day average and with a low RSI; a stock at its high scores 20 on that metric. Does the evidence support rewarding the discount, or does it point the other way?
- Finding: It points the other way over the horizons we hold for. (1) Momentum: stocks that rose most over the past 3 to 12 months kept beating the losers over the next 3 to 12 months (Jegadeesh and Titman, US, 1965 to 1989), and the effect shows up in eight markets and asset classes including UK shares (Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen, 2013). (2) Nearness to the 52-week high: stocks close to their high beat those far below it, this explains much of the momentum effect, and unlike plain momentum the gain does not reverse in later years (George and Hwang, 2004). The usual explanation is anchoring: investors are slow to bid a stock through its old high on good news. (3) What does favour buying a dip is short-term reversal: last month's losers tend to bounce the next month, but the effect is small in large caps, lasts weeks, and needs frequent trading to harvest (de Groot, Huij and Zhou, 2012). That is a swing-trading effect, not a reason to prefer a 5-year holding. Caveats: momentum suffers sharp crashes, mostly in rebounds after bear markets, and value (which our Value pillar measures) works against momentum; combining the two beats either alone (Asness and others).
- Strength: Strong for 12-month momentum (decades, many countries, published in top journals and still present out of sample). Moderate for the 52-week-high version (fewer replications). Weak for any benefit from a 20% to 30% discount on its own over a multi-year horizon.
- Sources: [George and Hwang, "The 52-Week High and Momentum Investing" (Journal of Finance, 2004), summary at CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/1284/technical-trading/the-52-week-high-as-a-momentum-indicator-for-individual-stocks/); [Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen, "Value and Momentum Everywhere" (Journal of Finance, 2013)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~lpederse/papers/ValMomEverywhere.pdf); [de Groot, Huij and Zhou, "Another look at trading costs and short-term reversal profits" (Journal of Banking and Finance, 2012)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbfina:v:36:y:2012:i:2:p:371-382); Jegadeesh and Titman, "Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers" (Journal of Finance, 1993).
- Implication: our Entry pillar is structurally anti-momentum, so "Good entry" leans towards stocks that have been falling for months (the 50/200-day "falling" guard only catches the worst cases). Proposed, not applied: (a) score "off 52-week high" flat between -3% and -20% and lower beyond -30%, instead of peaking at -30%; (b) add 12-1 month price momentum (12-month return excluding the last month) to the Entry pillar; (c) keep low RSI as a swing signal only. Before proposing it for verdicts, test it on our own data: forward 6 and 12-month returns of index stocks by distance from the 52-week high, in the same harness as the buy-tier backtest (roadmap). Any change alters verdicts and the pick tranche check, so it needs Douglas's OK.

## 2026-11-03: Is "extreme fear" on its own a reason to buy?
- Question: CNN Fear & Greed is at 23 (extreme fear) and the regime reads "Nervous, but no dip yet" with the S&P 500 only 3.5% off its high. Does low sentiment by itself predict better returns, or only alongside a real price fall (as our buy tiers assume)?
- Finding: The evidence is lopsided. High sentiment reliably predicts lower returns (Baker, Wurgler and Yuan, six markets 1980 to 2005: one standard deviation more sentiment, about 3.5% a year less on the market, far more for small, volatile and growth stocks). Low sentiment predicts little: Cheema, Man and Szulczyk (US, 1965 to 2015) found the sentiment index forecast returns only after high-sentiment periods, with no significant effect after low-sentiment ones. For CNN Fear & Greed itself, Farrell and O'Connor found it helped forecast S&P 500 returns in 2011 to 2020 but "considerably weaker" in 2021 to 2024, mostly driven by the years before 2014. Our own data since 2020 agrees: on days with F&G between 18 and 28 the S&P 500 did slightly better over 1 month (median +2.4% vs +1.6% on all days) but not over a year (+14.3% vs +16.0%, positive 58% vs 80% of the time; 24 episodes, heavily overlapping).
- Strength: Moderate that fear alone is not a reliable buy signal (several decades, several markets, consistent with our sample). Weak for any specific F&G threshold (short history, the index is built partly from price momentum, so it partly measures the drawdown anyway).
- Sources: [Baker, Wurgler and Yuan (2012) and Cheema, Man and Szulczyk (2020), summarised by The Evidence-Based Investor](https://www.evidenceinvestor.com/post/does-investor-sentiment-predict-market-movements); [Baker and Wurgler, "Investor Sentiment in the Stock Market" (NBER w13189)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w13189); [Farrell and O'Connor, "The CNN Fear and Greed Index as a predictor of US equity index returns" (Finance Research Letters, 2025)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finlet/v72y2025ics1544612324015216.html); own data, `data.json` context block (F&G vs S&P 500, Jul 2020 to Nov 2026).
- Implication: supports the current rules, no change proposed. F&G (and VIX) should stay a confirmation of a price-based tier, never a trigger on its own; the pullback rule (S&P 500 or FTSE 100 down 5%) and the -10% tier do the work. The "fear without a dip" heads-up stays informational. Worth adding to the buy-tier backtest (roadmap item 5): compare "tier hit" with "tier hit and F&G <= 25" to see whether the sentiment confirm adds anything.

## 2026-11-02: Should valuation be scored against sector peers, and how should cyclicals be treated?
- Question: the scan's top long-term score today is Sandisk (92, forward P/E about 4), with Western Digital (84, P/E about 9) close behind. Fixed P/E bands reward any low P/E. Is a low P/E a reliable signal for cyclical companies, and is a sector-relative score better?
- Finding: (1) Value measured within an industry predicts returns better than value measured across the whole market (Asness, Porter and Stevens, 1963 to 1998, all US stocks). Morningstar found the cheapest-industry tilt itself added nothing (-0.32% a year, 1966 to 2016) while stock-level value earned +2.36% a year: the return comes from picking cheap stocks within sectors, and industry bets mostly add risk. (2) For cyclicals, a low P/E often marks peak earnings: McKinsey found consensus forecasts for 36 US cyclicals kept trending up whether the company was at a peak or a trough, and prices track a blend of current and normalised earnings. Normalised (mid-cycle) earnings are the recommended anchor.
- Strength: Moderate to strong for sector-relative value (several decades, replicated, used by index and quant providers). Moderate for the cyclical point (smaller sample, but consistent with long practitioner experience).
- Sources: [Asness, Porter and Stevens, "Predicting Stock Returns Using Industry-Relative Firm Characteristics" (AQR working paper, 2000)](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Working-Paper/Predicting-Stock-Returns-Using-IndustryRelative-Firm-Characteristics); [Bryan and McCullough, "The Impact of Industry Tilts on Factor Performance" (Morningstar, 2017)](https://www.morningstar.co.uk/static/UploadManager/Assets/The_Impact_of_Industry_Tilts_on_Factor_Performance.pdf); [McKinsey, "How to value cyclical companies"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/how-to-value-cyclical-companies).
- Implication: supports roadmap item "sector-relative valuation". Proposed (not yet applied): score P/E and EV/EBITDA against the scan's sector median; for cyclical industries (memory and storage chips, miners, chemicals and fertilisers, autos, energy, airlines) add a "Cyclical: low P/E may be peak earnings" note and cap the value pillar so a P/E alone cannot make a "Good entry". This would change verdicts, which feed the pick tranche check (Newmont and CF Industries are cyclicals), so it needs Douglas's OK before it affects the plan check.

## 2026-11-01: Do the swing rules have an edge?
- Question: before real money goes into the swing pot on 16 Nov, would the agreed swing rules (scanner "Setup", entry next open, 2×ATR stop, 2R target, 20-session exit) have made money over the past year?
- Finding: No edge. 3,804 closed trades on S&P 500 and FTSE 100 stocks (Nov 2025 to Oct 2026): 42% winners, average **+0.04R** per trade (US +0.06R, UK -0.02R). A baseline that enters on any day with the same stops and targets did better: +0.10R. Higher setup scores helped a little (75+: +0.14R on 589 trades, still barely above the baseline). Results swung by month from -0.54R (Mar 2026) to +0.53R (Feb 2026). Trading 212's 0.15% FX fee each way on US shares costs roughly 0.05R to 0.1R per trade at typical stop widths, which wipes out the edge.
- Strength: Moderate. Large sample, but one year of a mostly rising market, today's index members only (survivorship), overlapping trades, and earnings dates not filtered historically.
- Sources: own backtest, `tools/swing-backtest.mjs`, run 1 Nov 2026 on Yahoo daily data.
- Implication: recommend keeping the swing pot on paper (live paper log started 30 Oct) rather than funding it on 16 Nov; revisit after 3 months of live paper results, or retune the setups (for example only score 75+ in an uptrend) and re-test. Decision for Douglas.

## 2026-10-30: Is the run-up to the midterms the strongest period for the S&P 500?
- Finding: No. Since 1948 the S&P 500 averaged ~4.6% in midterm years vs ~11.2% in the other three years; the strong window is the 12 months after the midterms (~13.9% average vs ~5.8% for comparable periods). 2026 has not followed the usual midterm weakness.
- Strength: Weak to moderate. Long record but only ~19 cycles; averages hide big variation; widely known, so may be partly priced in.
- Sources: Oppenheimer, "The Midterm Election Effect on Markets" (2026); Bank of America via investinglive.
- Implication: No rule change. Do not time the core on it. Mention as context in summaries around 3 Nov 2026.

## 2026-10-30: Lump sum or drip-feed the new money?
- Finding: Lump sum beat 12-month drip-feeding about two-thirds of the time in the UK and US; average gap ~2.2% (UK). Drip-feeding wins in steady falls and helps nervous investors stay invested.
- Strength: Strong (Vanguard study across decades and countries, replicated elsewhere).
- Sources: Vanguard, "Cost averaging: invest now or temporarily hold your cash?"; Monevator, "Lump sum investing versus drip feeding".
- Implication: Half the core now, the rest over three months (short stagger, not 12).

## 2026-10-30: Copying politicians and investors (Autopilot, NANC/KRUZ)
- Finding: Autopilot mirrors disclosed trades of US politicians (STOCK Act, up to 45 days late) and fund managers (13F, quarterly, up to 45 days after quarter end). Headline returns quoted are of the politicians' disclosed trades, not of app users. UK availability not confirmed. NANC beat the S&P in the year to June 2024 largely via one stock (Nvidia).
- Strength: Weak. Short histories, lags, survivorship and concentration effects.
- Sources: CGAA overview of Autopilot; etf.com NANC vs KRUZ; Gray News on 2026 Congress trading bills.
- Implication: Build as a tracked tie-breaker signal only (roadmap), drop after a year if it does not beat the S&P.
