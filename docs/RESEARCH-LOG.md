## 2026-11-12 (build session, afternoon): Does a results jump with an earnings beat drift further? (swing lab round 6)
- Question: Douglas asked to make the results feature "better for gains". The pot's drift setup spots results by volume, which round 5 found right only 36 to 43% of the time. Yahoo holds about ten years of real, timed results dates with the EPS forecast and actual (`fetchResultsHistory()`), so the drift literature could be tested on real results days. Pre-registered in `src/surpriseDrift.js` before the run: H8, results-day jumps of 4%+ with a 5%+ EPS beat clear the round-1 bar at 20 sessions (incl. 3 of 4 years and the same-month check) AND beat the pot's current trigger (4%+ on 3x volume, on results days) by 0.3+; H9, the current trigger on real results days beats the same trigger away from results by 0.3+ and in both halves; H7, the top fifth of surprise beats the bottom fifth against the index by 2+ points over 60 sessions, ahead in 3 of 4 years; D2, 60%+ of US events carry a surprise.
- Finding: D2 passes (97% of 18,267 US results days have a surprise). **H8 fails, narrowly**: jumps with a 5%+ beat (2,555) earned +1.59% a trade over 20 sessions after costs, +0.79 points over random holds, positive in both markets and both halves and against same-month holds (+0.76), but ahead in only 6 of 10 years (bar 8) and only +0.23 over the current trigger (bar 0.3). **H9 fails**: the current trigger on real results days (+0.56) did no better than volume shocks away from results (+0.87), so restricting the pot to results days would not help. **H7 fails**: top-minus-bottom surprise fifth +1.61 points at 60 sessions (bar 2), ahead in 7 of 10 years, in line with drift fading in large caps (research log 12 Nov morning). - Strength: Moderate. Ten years, about 19,000 results days with real dates and forecasts, but today's index members only, UK thin (44 jump-with-beat events), and year-to-year swings are large (2025: −0.70; 2020: +3.08).
- Sources: own backtest, run 12 Nov 2026 (`tools/surprise-drift.mjs`, branch `lab-runup`, `dry-run/surprise-drift.json`); Bernard and Thomas (1989); Livnat and Mendenhall (2006); Martineau, Critical Finance Review (2022).
- Implication: no rule change. The beat is now shown next to every results reaction on the Dashboard ("Just reported"), as context. Proposal for Douglas: when the pot has more drift signals than free slots, rank them by the size of the EPS beat (today: results days first, then volume). It changes only which signal takes a scarce slot, and the exploratory gap above is the reason; it did not clear the bar on its own. ## 2026-11-12 (build session): Do shares rise in the days before results? (swing lab round 5, the run-up)
- Question: Douglas asked for "a better signal on upcoming results and how shares will jump" after Burberry rose about 9% on its 12 Nov interims. The direction of the results-day move cannot be called in advance (rounds 2 and 4), so Claude tested the one pre-results effect with published support, the earnings announcement premium (research log 10 Nov): buy at the open 5 sessions before the results reaction day, sell at the last close before the news, never holding through it. Pre-registered in `src/runupStudy.js` before the run: H6, against random 5-session holds after costs, an edge of 0.4+ points a trade, a positive average, 300+ trades, ahead in 3 of 4 years, in each market and both halves (2017-2021, 2022-2026), and ahead of random holds started in the same calendar month (results cluster in season); D1, the results dates must be right.
- Run 1 (volume-found dates, as pre-registered): **D1 failed.** The busiest day 7 to 75 days after each quarter end, at 2x normal volume, matched only 43% of Yahoo's dates and 36% of the hand-checked dates; large caps often trade only 1.1 to 2x their normal volume on results (Newmont, VICI, HSBC). Verdict "dates unreliable", so its numbers (an edge of −0.42) say nothing about results.
- Run 2 (same H6, Yahoo's timed results dates, after-close reporters exit at that day's close; these matched 91% of the hand-checked dates): **H6 fails.** 18,946 run-ups: +0.10% a trade after costs against −0.08% for random 5-session holds, an edge of +0.17 (bar 0.4); ahead in 7 of 10 years (bar 8 of 10 here); positive in both markets (US +0.13, UK +0.32 on only 737) and both halves (+0.07, +0.27). The deciding check: against random holds started in the same month the edge is **0.00**, and against the home index the run-ups trailed by 0.18 points. Shares do drift up into results season, but no more than everything else at that time of year. Exploratory only: the 10-session window showed +0.38 (US) and +0.65 (UK) against random holds, before any same-month check; not a verdict, and not to be chased on this data (follow-up e).
- Strength: Moderate. Ten years, about 19,000 events, real dates; but today's index members only, Yahoo's dates thin for UK half-yearly reporters (737 UK events), and the US result matches the published finding that the premium shrank after about 2004 (Heitz, Narayanamoorthy and Zekhnini, 2020).
- Sources: own backtests, runs 12 Nov 2026 (`tools/runup-study.mjs`, branch `lab-runup`: `dry-run/runup-study.json` run 1, `dry-run/runup-study-2.json` run 2, `dry-run/runup-dates.json` date check); Frazzini and Lamont, NBER w13090 (2007); Barber, De George, Lehavy and Trueman, Journal of Financial Economics (2013); Heitz, Narayanamoorthy and Zekhnini (2020 working paper).
- Implication: no new setup. The run-up joins the bounce as a tested idea that does not clear the bar; the short-term pot keeps its two setups. What did ship is display and heads-up only: the results preview (usual results-day move from real dates, a push a week out and the day before). Side finding: any future study or display needing results days should use Yahoo's timed dates (`fetchReportedDates()`), not the busiest-day guess; the existing `resultsMoves()` fallback (picks' "Last results" line) uses that guess when no hand date exists and is worth moving over.

## 2026-11-12 (daily run): How much of a backtested edge survives live, and can 30 trades tell? (short-term pot review)
- Question: the short-term pot trades real money from today on two published effects (results drift, short-term bounces). The agreed review drops a setup that trails random entry after costs once it has 30 closed live trades. How much of the backtest's edge should we expect live, and is 30 trades enough to judge it?
- Finding: published edges shrink. McLean and Pontiff (Journal of Finance, 2016; 97 anomalies) find returns about 26% lower out of sample and about 58% lower after publication, the gap being investors trading on the papers. Post-earnings drift is the clearest case: Martineau (Critical Finance Review, 2022) finds it gone in large US stocks since about 2006, with prices taking in the surprise on the day; Subrahmanyam (2025, US data 2001 to 2024) finds the drift factor's t-statistic falls from 2.18 to 1.43 once microcaps are left out, against two 2025 papers (Hirshleifer, Peng and Wang; Dickerson, Julliard and Mueller) that say it lives on. Our own backtest is already post-2006 and large caps only (S&P 500, FTSE 100, 2017 to 2026), and found +0.58 points a trade over random entry, ahead in 6 of 9 years: in line with "small and fragile". On sample size, from our own backtest: a 20-session drift trade has a spread of about 12 points, so the average of 30 trades has a 90% range of about plus or minus 3.6 points, six times the whole edge. A setup with exactly the backtest's edge would still trail random entry at 30 trades about 40% of the time (bounces: 41%; spread 3.7, edge 0.16); checked by simulation with fat-tailed returns (39%). Telling the edge apart from zero at 90% confidence needs about 1,160 drift trades or 1,460 bounces.
- Strength: strong on decay (large, replicated samples); mixed on whether drift survives in large caps; the sample-size arithmetic is exact given our backtest's spread.
- Sources: [McLean and Pontiff, Does academic research destroy stock return predictability?](https://ivey.uwo.ca/media/3775549/pontiff.pdf); [Martineau, Rest in peace post-earnings announcement drift (CFR)](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/CFR-0122); [UCLA Anderson Review, Is post-earnings announcement drift a thing again? (Subrahmanyam 2025)](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/is-post-earnings-announcement-drift-a-thing-again); our `data/swing-lab.json` (spreads and edges).
- Implication for our rules: expect the live edge to be about half the backtest's or less, which for drift is roughly the size of the noise in costs. The 30-trade review as agreed is close to a coin toss: it would drop a working setup about 4 times in 10. Proposed change (for Douglas): at 30 trades, drop a setup only if it is *clearly* behind random entry (the top of its 90% range below random), or if it trails at all after 100 trades; and keep the pot's 25% pause as the real safety net. Shipped display only: the Dashboard card now shows each setup's review line with its 90% range and these odds. Rule unchanged until agreed.

## 2026-11-11 (daily run): Does today's US mega-cap concentration mean lower returns for the core?
- Question: VWRP is about 60% US, and its biggest names (Nvidia ~5%, then Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta) are a large slice of it. The S&P 500 is near its most concentrated since 1926. Should that change the core, or argue for a bigger tilt away in the picks pot?
- Finding: concentration has not predicted returns. Vanguard (Yan and Plagge, 2026; 18 MSCI markets, 2006 to 2025, 330 market-years with market and year fixed effects) finds no significant link between concentration (the "effective number" of stocks) and next-year return, Sharpe ratio or the chance of a 10% or 20% fall; only a small rise in volatility (about 0.23 points per standard deviation of concentration, against a median of 17%). On US data since 1926, the correlation between the effective number of stocks and the next decade's return is only 0.15. PWL Capital (Kerzérho, June 2026) puts the S&P 500's effective number at about 45, similar to 1926, 1957 and 1981, and cites "The Fallacy of Concentration" (Financial Analysts Journal): timing equity exposure on concentration did not beat a constant mix in 4 of 6 countries. UBS Global Investment Returns Yearbook 2024 (Dimson, Marsh, Staunton): the US top 10 were 25% of its market, less than the UK (36%), France (55%) or Germany (57%), so the US is concentrated by its own history, not by world standards; the US share of world markets rose from 41% (2010) to about 60%.
- Strength: moderate. Two independent analyses over many markets agree there is no reliable return signal; the samples are short for the cross-market work (20 years), and long-run US evidence is one country.
- Sources: [Vanguard, On the link between concentration in stock index portfolios and returns](https://fund-docs.vanguard.com/VIA_Market_Concentration_OCT26.pdf); [PWL Capital, The US stock market is concentrated. So what?](https://pwlcapital.com/the-us-stock-market-is-concentrated-so-what/); [Cambridge Judge, US equity market dominance (UBS Yearbook 2024)](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/2024/us-equity-market-dominance-why-its-not-just-the-uk-losing-out/).
- Implication for our rules: no change. VWRP already holds the market at its weights, and the practical answer both sources give to concentration worries (more non-US exposure) is what VWRP's ~40% outside the US and the picks pot's tilt away from US mega-cap tech already do. It argues against a timing rule on concentration, and against adding a second "ex-US" fund to the core: the evidence does not say it would earn more, only that it would hold differently. Keep the look-through of the biggest single companies in the pots panel (a risk view, not a signal), and keep Meta as the only mega-cap pick.

## 2026-11-11 (night): What do Warren Buffett's principles say, and how can we test them? (Buffett lens)
- Question: Douglas (11 Nov): "lean into Warren Buffett's principles on picking companies and investment". What are the principles, what is the evidence they work beyond Buffett himself, and how can we apply them without a backtest?
- Principles, in his words: buy wonderful businesses at fair prices ("far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price", 1989 letter); durable competitive advantage shown by high returns on capital without much debt (1977 to 1987 letters); judge earnings by "owner earnings", profit plus depreciation minus the capital spending needed to keep the business (1986 letter); insist on a margin of safety (from Graham, The Intelligent Investor, chapter 20); stay within your circle of competence; hold for a very long time ("our favourite holding period is forever", 1988 letter). And for most people: a low-cost index fund (2013 letter: 90% in an S&P 500 index fund), which is what our 70% core already is.
- Evidence: Frazzini, Kabiller and Pedersen, "Buffett's Alpha" (Financial Analysts Journal, 2018): Berkshire's returns from 1976 are largely explained by buying cheap, safe, high-quality companies (their "quality minus junk" factor plus low risk and value), held with modest cheap leverage; a systematic portfolio of such stocks tracked his public-stock returns closely. Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen, "Quality minus junk" (Review of Accounting Studies, 2019): profitable, steady, well-run companies earned more than junk across 24 countries. Novy-Marx (2013): gross profitability predicts returns as strongly as value. So the quality tests have broad support; the edge is moderate and comes with long dry spells (2017 to 2020 for value).
- Method (written in `src/buffett.js` before any result was seen): six quality tests on the last four annual accounts (Yahoo fundamentals timeseries, free): return on capital 15%+ average and never under 10% (banks and insurers: return on equity 12%+, 10% minimum); gross margin 40%+ and steady; a profit every year with EPS up; long-term debt under 4 years of profit; owner earnings 80%+ of profit; no dilution. Value: owner earnings grown at past EPS growth capped at 7% for 10 years, then 2.5%, discounted at 10%, computed on the earnings yield so pence and dollars cannot mix; "buy zone" needs a 25%+ discount. REITs, investment trusts and holding companies are not assessed. Checked on four companies' real accounts: Coca-Cola and Apple pass all six, JPMorgan passes the three bank tests, HSBC fails on return on equity (11.4% average, 8.7% worst).
- Strength: the principles are well supported; our version is a simplification (four years of accounts, no judgement of management or competitive position, a crude intrinsic value). It cannot be backtested here, because free data stops at four years and today's accounts know the future.
- Implication: no rule change. Display-only Buffett lens on every assessed company (Research page, two filters), and a Buffett book run forward on paper from December (10 holdings at most, buy zone only, sold only if a test fails or the price runs 50% above value). After a year, compare it with VWRP and the picks pot; if it does well, propose using the quality tests as a gate for new picks.

## 2026-11-11 (night): Does stock momentum, held a month at a time, beat holding every member? (swing lab round 5)
- Question: Douglas asked to develop the swing side. Rounds 1 to 4 found no short-term edge after costs. Momentum is the best-documented anomaly and trades far less than a 20-session swing. Pre-registered in `src/momentum.js` before the run: for the S&P 500 and the FTSE 100 separately, each month hold the 10 members with the best 12-1 month return, equal weight, in cash when the index is below its 10-month average; Trading 212 costs (US 0.3%, UK 0.5% round trip) on turnover; against every member equally weighted. Bar: 2+ points a year in both markets, both halves (split Jan 2016), ahead in 60%+ of 3-year spells, worst fall no more than 5 points deeper. Reality check: MTUM against SPY.
- Method: `tools/momentum-study.mjs` on the dryrun runner, Yahoo adjusted closes (dividends in), 499 S&P 500 and 100 FTSE 100 members, Dec 2007 to Oct 2026 (227 months).
- Finding: the primary **passes the bar in both markets**. S&P 500: 28.9% a year vs 15.9% for all members (+13.0 points), ahead in 93% of 3-year spells, both halves (+10.1, +15.3), worst fall 28% vs 45%, in cash 23% of months, turnover about 7 times a year. FTSE 100: 11.6% vs 9.3% (+2.4), ahead in 63% of spells, halves +5.1 and +0.4, worst fall 18% vs 37%. The real fund's +0.8 points is the better guide to what is left. The UK result just clears the bar and is near zero since 2016. The trend filter's drawdown cut is the most robust part (it also showed in round 3).
- Sources: own backtest, run 11 Nov 2026 (`data/momentum-study.json`). Jegadeesh and Titman, Journal of Finance (1993); Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen, "Value and Momentum Everywhere", Journal of Finance (2013); Daniel and Moskowitz, "Momentum crashes", Journal of Financial Economics (2016); Faber, "A Quantitative Approach to Tactical Asset Allocation" (2007).
- Implication: no rule change and no real money. It earns a live paper test, which has no survivorship: a monthly "momentum book" (top 10 by 12-1 month return, trend filter, costs) beside the swing and drift books, judged by the same proposed bar. Next up in the roadmap.

## 2026-11-10 (morning): What should we expect from a five-stock picks pot, compared with the index?
- Question: the picks pot holds five companies (15% of the portfolio). What does the long-run evidence say about the odds that a handful of stocks keeps up with the market, and should the plan measure the pot against VWRP?
- Finding: (1) Most single stocks lose to cash over their lives: 58% of US stocks (1926 to 2016) returned less than one-month Treasury bills, and 4.3% of companies (1,092 of about 26,000) produced all the market's net gain over bills; 86 produced half (Bessembinder 2018). (2) The same holds worldwide: of over 64,000 stocks (1990 to 2020), 55% of US and 57% of non-US stocks trailed bills, and 2.4% of companies produced all $75.7 trillion of net wealth creation (Bessembinder, Chen, Choi and Wei 2023). (3) Vanguard (Russell 3000, 1990 to 2025): about 75% of stocks trailed the index; the median stock returned -6.6% in total against an average of +575%; in random-portfolio simulations a one-stock holding's median return trailed the index by about 13 points a year, and the gap narrows as holdings are added. In Bessembinder's bootstrap a single random stock lagged the value-weighted market in 96% of runs over 90 years. (4) The reason is skew: a few huge winners lift the average while the typical stock lags, so a small portfolio's most likely result is below the index even if its average is not.
- Strength: Strong. Very large samples, decades, US and global, replicated by independent groups (CFA Institute journal, Vanguard). It describes random picks; it does not say whether our score picks better than random (our track record is too young to tell: 7 scored signals).
- Sources: [Bessembinder, "Do stocks outperform Treasury bills?" (Journal of Financial Economics, 2018), ASU summary](https://news.wpcarey.asu.edu/20180917-do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills); [Bessembinder, Chen, Choi and Wei, "Long-Term Shareholder Returns: Evidence from 64,000 Global Stocks" (Financial Analysts Journal, 2023)](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2023/long-term-shareholder-returns-evidence-from-64000-global-stocks); [Vanguard, "The power of diversification" (Oct 2026)](https://www.vanguard.co.uk/content/dam/intl/europe/documents/en/the-power-of-diversification-oct-2026.pdf); [Swedroe, Alpha Architect, on Bessembinder's 2021 update](https://alphaarchitect.com/?p=66923).
- Implication: the 15% cap and the 3% per-stock limit are the right shape: they bound the cost of the likely outcome (the pot trails) while keeping some exposure to a big winner. Two proposals for Douglas (not applied): (a) a yearly scorecard at the Sunday review from 2 Nov 2027: the picks pot's return against the same money in VWRP, including FX fees; (b) a review rule: if after three years (Nov 2029) the pot has trailed VWRP, fold the picks pot into the core. No change to the picks themselves; replacing a laggard on price alone would be a momentum bet the evidence above does not support.

## 2026-11-10 (daily run, evening): Does the size of the earnings surprise predict what follows? (swing lab round 4)

Most of the spread is the bottom fifth (misses or bare beats, under about +0.9%) trailing by 4.4 points; the other four fifths are close together. **H5 passes**: after a rise, strong surprises +1.6 vs −1.5 for other rises (+3.1); after a fall, weak surprises −3.6 vs +0.5 for other falls (−4.1). - Sources: own backtest, run 10 Nov 2026 (`data/surprise-study.json`); Bernard and Thomas, Journal of Accounting Research (1989); Livnat and Mendenhall, "Comparing the Post-Earnings Announcement Drift for Surprises Calculated from Analyst and Time Series Forecasts", Journal of Accounting Research (2006); Meta's Q3 2026 results (investor.atmeta.com; Newsquawk headline: EPS 3.83 vs 6.67 expected).
- Implication: no rule change. Display only: the scan now records each pick's, bench name's and results-day mover's latest surprise and reaction ("Last results" on the Research page, `data/results-check.json`), flagging "weak results and a falling price" and "strong results and a rising price" with the group's past 60-session result. As of 10 Nov that flags HSBC (a pick: EPS 3.9% under forecast, −5.4% on the day), NatWest, CF and Amazon (bench). Proposal for Douglas, not a rule: when a pick is flagged "weak results and a falling price", hold its next tranche until the next results, even if it shows Good entry. Rerun the study each quarter (`node tools/surprise-study.mjs` on the runners) so the evidence grows from one year.

## 2026-11-10 (daily run, later): Is post-results drift real, and should a pick wait after bad results? (swing lab round 2)
- Question: round 1 found drift (+4% on 3× volume) the one near-miss at 20 sessions. Hypotheses written in `src/driftStudy.js` before the run: H1, held 60 sessions (Bernard and Thomas: drift runs up to about 60 trading days) it clears the round-1 bar; H2, the 20-session edge is positive in both halves (2017 to 2021, 2022 to 2026) and both markets; H3, after a −4% day on 3× volume the next 20 and 60 sessions do worse than random by 0.4+ points (if so, a pick tranche should wait after bad results). Exploratory cuts (move size, opening gap, index trend) are shown but carry no verdict.
- Method: `tools/drift-study.mjs`, same 598 stocks and costs as round 1, Nov 2017 to Nov 2026: 2,777 up days, 3,654 down days, 132,436 random days, forward returns at 5, 10, 20, 40 and 60 sessions from the next open. After the first run, a placebo check was added (marked as added afterwards in the code): each event against random days in the same calendar month, and against the same stock's random days.
- Finding: **H1 passes as written**: held 60 sessions, up days return +6.57% after costs vs +2.98% for random days (+3.58 points, ahead in 8 of 9 years, US +3.51 and UK +3.78). **H2 passes**: +0.34 points in 2017 to 2021, +0.96 in 2022 to 2026. **But the placebo undercuts both**: down days on heavy volume also beat random by +2.75 points at 60 sessions. Against random days in the same month, jumps return +2.92 and falls +2.34 at 60 sessions, so drift proper (jump minus fall) is only +0.13 at 20 sessions, +0.80 at 40 and +0.58 at 60; against the same stock's own random days it is +0.24, +0.46 and −0.48. Most of the apparent edge is a volume or volatility effect common to both directions (in line with the high-volume return premium, Gervais, Kaniel and Mingelgrin 2001), plus survivorship: volatile stocks that are still in today's index are the ones that recovered, which a backtest of today's members cannot avoid. 2020 alone contributes a lot (+11 points at 60 sessions). **H3 fails**: after a heavy-volume fall, the next 20 sessions were level with random (+0.08) and the next 60 ahead of it (+2.75), behind random in only 4 and 3 of 9 years. Exploratory: moves of +12% or more did best (+2.81 at 20 sessions, 782 events) and +7 to 12% worst (−0.47); untested as a rule, likely noise.
- Strength: Moderate on H3 (large sample, both markets; consistent with the 10 Nov sell-signal study, where an 8% fall did not help holders). Weak on drift itself: the directional part is small and unstable once timing and volatility are matched, and survivorship cannot be removed from this backtest.
- Sources: own backtest, run 10 Nov 2026 (`data/drift-study.json`; `dry-run/drift-study.json` on the `dryrun-lab` branch). Bernard and Thomas, Journal of Accounting Research (1989); Gervais, Kaniel and Mingelgrin, "The High-Volume Return Premium", Journal of Finance (2001).
- Implication: no rule change. (1) For picks: no case for a "wait after bad results" rule; the 7-day before-results rule stays as it is. (2) Swing stays on paper. The live paper test now tracks heavy-volume moves in both directions, valued at 20 sessions and closed at 60, against the home index; a forward test has no survivorship bias, so up minus down there is the honest measure of drift. ## 2026-11-10 (daily run): Does skipping a pick tranche in the 7 days before results cost returns? (earnings announcement premium)
- Question: the agreed rule never buys a pick tranche within 7 days before results. Is there evidence that stocks drift up or down into results, so the rule gives up (or saves) return?
- Finding: historically there was an *earnings announcement premium*: stocks earned more in the weeks and months they reported than at other times (US 1927 to 2002: about 7 to 18% a year annualised for an announcement-month portfolio, Frazzini and Lamont; 9.9% a year abnormal, Savor and Wilson; 46 countries 1991 to 2010: a 7.2% a year long/short, Barber, De George, Lehavy and Trueman). Explanations: extra risk carried through the announcement, and attention-driven buying before it. More recent work finds the US premium shrank sharply after about 2004 (about 33 basis points a week before, under 10 after; Heitz, Narayanamoorthy and Zekhnini, 2020) while a UK premium was still present. Announcement windows also carry much higher stock-specific volatility; our own picks show the size: HSBC fell about 6% on its 27 Oct results day and Newmont 7.1% close to close around 22 Oct.
- Strength: Strong that the premium existed (several large, independent studies, many countries); moderate that it has largely faded in the US; the size is small next to a single results-day move.
- Sources: Frazzini and Lamont, "The Earnings Announcement Premium and Trading Volume", NBER w13090 (2007); Savor and Wilson, "Earnings Announcements and Systematic Risk", Journal of Finance (2016); Barber, De George, Lehavy and Trueman, "The earnings announcement premium around the globe", Journal of Financial Economics (2013), summary at Alpha Architect (https://alphaarchitect.com/2016/09/28/introducing-the-global-earnings-announcement-premium/); Heitz, Narayanamoorthy and Zekhnini, "The Disappearing Earnings Announcement Premium" (2020 working paper, https://www.iimb.ac.in/ARC2020/Papers/The_Disappearing_Earnings_Announcement_Premium.pdf); HSBC reaction: Khaleej Times / Hargreaves Lansdown, 28 Oct 2026.
- Implication: the 7-day rule is not a return rule. If anything, buying just before results earned slightly more on average in the past, but that edge is small (and faded in the US) next to the risk of a 6 to 7% gap on a third of a position. The rule's real job is to avoid a fresh, unknown result being priced in the day after buying, and to let the score update on the new numbers before a tranche; that is reasonable for a long-term pick. No change proposed; the playback should not describe the rule as protecting returns.

## 2026-11-05: Does holding a cash reserve to buy dips beat investing it straight away?
- Question: the plan keeps 15% of the ISA as a dip reserve in cash, deployed a third at each confirmed buy tier (-10%, -15/-20%, -30% with fear confirmation), with half moving to the core if unused after 12 months. What does the evidence say about the cost of waiting for dips?

## 2026-11-04: Does buying a stock well below its 52-week high improve long-term returns?
- Question: the long-term score's Entry pillar (26% of the score, and "Good entry" needs Entry 70+) gives the most points to stocks 20% to 30% below their 52-week high, below their 200-day average and with a low RSI; a stock at its high scores 20 on that metric. Does the evidence support rewarding the discount, or does it point the other way?
- Finding: It points the other way over the horizons we hold for. (1) Momentum: stocks that rose most over the past 3 to 12 months kept beating the losers over the next 3 to 12 months (Jegadeesh and Titman, US, 1965 to 1989), and the effect shows up in eight markets and asset classes including UK shares (Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen, 2013). (2) Nearness to the 52-week high: stocks close to their high beat those far below it, this explains much of the momentum effect, and unlike plain momentum the gain does not reverse in later years (George and Hwang, 2004). The usual explanation is anchoring: investors are slow to bid a stock through its old high on good news. (3) What does favour buying a dip is short-term reversal: last month's losers tend to bounce the next month, but the effect is small in large caps, lasts weeks, and needs frequent trading to harvest (de Groot, Huij and Zhou, 2012). That is a swing-trading effect, not a reason to prefer a 5-year holding. Caveats: momentum suffers sharp crashes, mostly in rebounds after bear markets, and value (which our Value pillar measures) works against momentum; combining the two beats either alone (Asness and others).
- Strength: Strong for 12-month momentum (decades, many countries, published in top journals and still present out of sample). Moderate for the 52-week-high version (fewer replications). Weak for any benefit from a 20% to 30% discount on its own over a multi-year horizon.
- Sources: [George and Hwang, "The 52-Week High and Momentum Investing" (Journal of Finance, 2004), summary at CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/1284/technical-trading/the-52-week-high-as-a-momentum-indicator-for-individual-stocks/); [Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen, "Value and Momentum Everywhere" (Journal of Finance, 2013)](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~lpederse/papers/ValMomEverywhere.pdf); [de Groot, Huij and Zhou, "Another look at trading costs and short-term reversal profits" (Journal of Banking and Finance, 2012)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbfina:v:36:y:2012:i:2:p:371-382); Jegadeesh and Titman, "Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers" (Journal of Finance, 1993).

## 2026-10-30: Is the run-up to the midterms the strongest period for the S&P 500?
- Finding: No. Since 1948 the S&P 500 averaged ~4.6% in midterm years vs ~11.2% in the other three years; the strong window is the 12 months after the midterms (~13.9% average vs ~5.8% for comparable periods). 2026 has not followed the usual midterm weakness.
- Strength: Weak to moderate. Long record but only ~19 cycles; averages hide big variation; widely known, so may be partly priced in.
- Sources: Oppenheimer, "The Midterm Election Effect on Markets" (2026); Bank of America via investinglive.
- Implication: No rule change. Do not time the core on it. Mention as context in summaries around 3 Nov 2026.

## 2026-10-30: Lump sum or drip-feed the new money?
- Finding: Lump sum beat 12-month drip-feeding about two-thirds of the time in the UK and US; average gap ~2.2% (UK). Drip-feeding wins in steady falls and helps nervous investors stay invested.
- Strength: Strong (Vanguard study across decades and countries, replicated elsewhere).
- Sources: Vanguard, "Cost averaging: invest now or temporarily hold your cash?"; Monevator, "Lump sum investing versus drip feeding".
- Implication: Half the core now, the rest over three months (short stagger, not 12).
